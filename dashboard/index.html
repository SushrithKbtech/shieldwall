        How ShieldWall protects a server

        Think of ShieldWall as a bouncer at the door of a game server. It watches everyone arriving, notices when a mob shows up, works out who they are, keeps them out, and then tells the owner what happened in plain English. No training data is needed: it learns what "normal" looks like from the first few seconds of real traffic.

        1. 1

          Count

          A tiny program inside the Linux kernel (eBPF/XDP) looks at every packet the moment it arrives and adds it to counters.

        2. 2

          Detect

          Once a second, three alarms compare the counters with normal. If two agree for two seconds, it is an incident.

        3. 3

          Identify

          A fingerprint check names the attack, or decides it is just a crowd of real players.

        4. 4

          Block

          A rule is written back into the kernel, which drops matching packets before they cost anything. Rules expire after 60 s.

        5. 5

          Explain

          An LLM turns the facts into a short summary and answers questions. It never decides what to block.

        The models, in plain words

        live values update every second
        Detect

        Spike alarm

        EWMA baseline + z-score

        "Is traffic far above normal right now?"

        Keeps a running average of normal traffic and measures how many "normal wobbles" away the current second is. Normal wobble is under 3; an attack is often over 1,000.

        Like a shop owner who knows a normal day has 100 customers and notices 10,000 walk in.

        Now: 0 (normal level 0 pps)
        Detect

        Build-up alarm

        CUSUM

        "Has traffic been creeping up?"

        Adds up every small rise above normal. One small rise is ignored, but a steady climb adds up until it trips the alarm.

        Like noticing a slow leak because the water bill keeps going up.

        Now: 0 (alarm at 10)
        Detect

        Stranger alarm

        Shannon entropy

        "Are lots of strangers suddenly showing up?"

        Measures how spread out the senders are. 300 regular players give a steady value; a botnet of thousands makes it jump.

        Like a regular café where a busload of strangers suddenly walks in.

        Now: 0 bits
        Count

        Three memory-saving counters

        Count-Min · HyperLogLog · Bloom

        "Who sends the most? How many senders? Seen them before?"

        Count-Min Sketch finds the biggest senders, HyperLogLog counts unique senders in 1 KB, and a Bloom filter remembers who it has seen. Millions of senders, a few kilobytes of memory.

        Now: 0 senders, 0% of traffic from never-seen senders
        Identify

        Fingerprint check

        rule-based classifier

        "What kind of attack is this?"

        Each attack leaves a tell-tale sign. Traffic coming from port 389, which this server never uses, means CLDAP amplification. Many connection requests that never finish means a SYN flood. Normal handshakes and a normal mix mean real players.

        Now: nothing unusual
        Block

        Mitigation controller + XDP filter

        expiring rules in BPF maps

        "What should we drop, and for how long?"

        Clean fingerprints get an exact drop rule (for example "UDP from port 389"). Messier floods get a speed limit for unknown senders, while known players pass. Rules expire after 60 s unless the attack continues. Admin addresses are never blocked.

        Now: no rules
        Explain

        AI assistant

        LLM

        "What happened, in words a non-expert understands?"

        Gets only verified facts (attack type, size, timings) and the event log, then writes 2-3 sentences or answers a question. It runs in the background, so blocking never waits for it, and it never blocks anything itself.

        Checked: - of the numbers it writes match the facts

        Why statistics instead of a trained ML model?

        • No training data needed. It learns your server's normal traffic in seconds and keeps adapting as the server grows.
        • Explainable. Every alert can say exactly which alarm fired and why, which an LLM can then put into words.
        • Fast. The maths runs in microseconds; the expensive part happens in the kernel.

        How these numbers were measured

        • Ground truth: the lab traffic generator knows exactly when each attack runs, so every second and every packet can be marked right or wrong.
        • 60 random trials: CLDAP, DNS, NTP, SYN and UDP attacks from 3,000 to 300,000 packets per second, plus flash crowds and quiet periods.
        • Why recall is below 100% per second: ShieldWall waits 2 seconds before acting so one odd second never blocks anyone. Those first seconds count as misses. That wait is why precision is 100%.
